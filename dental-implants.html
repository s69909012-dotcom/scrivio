A Simple Guide to Dental Implants

Learn what dental implants are, how the treatment works, what recovery can be like, and what a dentist may consider before recommending an implant.

Understanding Implants

What Is a Dental Implant?

A dental implant is a small post, usually made from titanium, that is placed in the jawbone to replace the root of a missing tooth. It can support a crown, bridge or other replacement tooth.

Implant Fixture

The part placed in the jawbone. It acts as an artificial tooth root.

Abutment

A connector that joins the implant fixture to the replacement tooth.

Crown

The visible replacement tooth. Depending on the treatment, an implant may instead support a bridge or denture.

Missing Teeth

Why Replace a Missing Tooth?

The best choice depends on the person and the condition of the mouth. Replacing a missing tooth may help with everyday function and may also help maintain the position of nearby teeth.

Chewing: Replacing a missing tooth may make chewing easier.

Tooth position: A gap can allow nearby teeth to move over time.

Appearance: A replacement tooth can fill a visible gap when appearance is an important part of treatment.

Tooth Replacement

From a Missing Tooth to a Replacement

An implant is one way to replace a missing tooth. Whether it is appropriate depends on the individual assessment.

Treatment Choices

What Are the Treatment Options?

Dental implants are one option for replacing missing teeth. Depending on the situation, a dentist may also discuss a bridge or removable denture.

Dental Implant

A replacement tooth supported by an implant placed in the jawbone.

Dental Bridge

A replacement tooth or teeth supported by neighbouring teeth or other structures.

Removable Denture

A removable appliance used to replace one or more missing teeth.

Implant Options

What Can Dental Implants Replace?

Implant treatment can be planned for one missing tooth, several missing teeth, or more extensive tooth loss.

Single Tooth Implant

One implant can support a replacement crown when one tooth is missing and the surrounding conditions are suitable.

Multiple Teeth Implants

Several missing teeth may be replaced using one or more implants, depending on the treatment plan.

More Extensive Tooth Loss

When many or all teeth are missing, implants may sometimes be used to support a bridge or removable denture. The exact plan depends on the condition of the mouth.

Implant Component

What Is the Implant Fixture?

The implant fixture is the part placed inside the jawbone. It acts as an artificial tooth root and provides support for the replacement tooth or other implant-supported restoration.

Healing

How Does the Implant Join the Bone?

During healing, the bone gradually grows around and bonds with the implant surface. This process is often called osseointegration.

During Treatment

What Happens During Implant Surgery?

The exact sequence can vary. For a straightforward implant, the procedure is usually carried out with local anaesthesia, so the area is numb while you remain awake. Implant placement is commonly an outpatient procedure, meaning you can usually go home afterwards.

01

Assessment

The dentist examines your mouth, gums, teeth, bite and available jawbone.

02

Imaging

X-rays or other imaging may be used to understand the bone and nearby structures.

03

Planning

The dentist plans the implant position and the type of replacement tooth needed.

04

Anaesthesia

Local anaesthesia is normally used to numb the treatment area.

05

Implant Placement

The gum is opened, a space is prepared in the jawbone and the implant fixture is placed.

06

Healing

The implant is given time to heal and bond with the bone.

07

Replacement Tooth

Once healing is sufficient, the abutment and crown, bridge or denture can be fitted as planned.

08

Ongoing Care

Regular cleaning and dental reviews help monitor the implant and the replacement tooth.

Recovery

What Can Recovery Be Like?

Recovery differs depending on the number of implants and whether other procedures were performed at the same time.

First Few Days

Soreness, swelling or bruising can occur. Swelling is often greatest during the first couple of days.

First 1–2 Weeks

The gum usually continues to heal during this period. Stitches may be removed or may dissolve, depending on the type used.

Bone Healing

Bone healing takes longer than gum healing. The implant may need several months before it is ready to support the final replacement tooth.

Temporary teeth: Depending on the treatment, a temporary tooth, temporary bridge or denture may be used while the implant area heals. Whether this is possible depends on the individual case.

When to contact a dental professional: Contact the treating dental professional if pain, swelling or bleeding is severe, gets worse instead of improving, or if you develop signs of infection or another problem that concerns you.

Before Treatment

What Does a Dentist Check?

An implant is not suitable simply because a tooth is missing. A dentist will look at several parts of your mouth and overall health before deciding whether it is appropriate.

Jawbone

Amount and condition of available bone.

Gum Health

Signs of gum disease or inflammation.

Nearby Teeth

Condition of teeth around the missing tooth.

Bite

How your upper and lower teeth meet.

Oral Hygiene

How well the teeth and gums are being cleaned.

Medical Factors

Relevant health conditions, medicines and healing factors.

Before You Choose

Things to Keep in Mind

These are factors a dental professional may consider when assessing whether implant treatment is appropriate.

Bone: There needs to be enough suitable bone to place and support the implant. If there is not, additional treatment may sometimes be discussed.

Gum disease: Active gum disease may need to be treated before implant surgery.

Smoking: Smoking can affect healing and is associated with a higher risk of implant problems.

Healing conditions: Some medical conditions, medicines or previous treatment can affect healing and need to be discussed with the dental team.

Oral hygiene: Good cleaning around the implant and regular dental care are important after treatment.

Teeth grinding: Strong clenching or grinding can place extra force on implants and replacement teeth.

Bone Support

What Is Bone Grafting?

If there is not enough suitable bone at the implant site, a dentist may sometimes recommend a bone graft.

A bone graft is a procedure used to add or rebuild bone in an area of the jaw. The added material provides a framework where new bone can develop. The graft may be done before implant placement or, in some cases, at the same time.

Bone grafting can add extra treatment and healing time. The type of graft and timing depend on how much bone is available, where the implant is planned and the individual situation.

Risks & Considerations

What Are the Risks of Implant Surgery?

Dental implant surgery is a procedure, so it has possible risks. Your dental professional should explain the risks that apply specifically to you before treatment.

Soreness & Swelling

Temporary pain, swelling or bruising can occur after surgery.

Infection

Infection can occur around a surgical site or around an implant, particularly if oral hygiene and gum health are poor.

Implant Problems

In some cases an implant may not bond properly with the bone or may develop problems later.

Gum or Bone Changes

Inflammation around an implant can affect the surrounding gum and bone and may require further treatment.

Replacement Tooth Wear

Crowns, bridges, dentures or their small connecting parts can wear, chip or need repair or replacement over time.

Nerve or Nearby Structure Injury

This is uncommon, but surgery in certain areas of the jaw can affect nearby nerves or other structures.

If you develop severe or worsening pain, increasing swelling, significant bleeding, fever, or another symptom that concerns you after surgery, contact the treating dental professional promptly.

Long-Term Considerations

How Long Do Dental Implants Last?

Implants can function for many years, but there is no guarantee that an implant or its replacement tooth will last for life. Long-term results vary between patients.

Factors That Can Affect Longevity

  • Gum and bone health
  • Regular brushing and cleaning
  • Smoking or tobacco use
  • Bite forces and teeth grinding
  • General health and healing
  • Regular dental reviews

The Replacement Tooth May Need Care Too

  • Crowns can wear or chip
  • Bridges can require repair
  • Denture attachments can wear
  • Replacement parts may sometimes be needed
  • Regular maintenance remains important

After Treatment

Looking After an Implant

An implant still needs regular care after treatment is complete.

Clean regularly: Brush and clean between your teeth as advised by your dental team.

Attend reviews: Regular dental visits can help identify gum, bone or restoration problems early.

Avoid tobacco: Discuss smoking or tobacco use with your dental professional, because it can affect healing and long-term implant health.

Report changes: Tell your dental professional if the implant or replacement tooth feels loose, painful or different from before.

Common Questions

Dental Implant FAQ

The area is usually numbed with local anaesthesia during implant placement. Some soreness, swelling or bruising can occur after surgery. Your dental professional can advise you about pain relief and what to expect based on the procedure.

The total treatment time varies from person to person. The gum may heal within a few weeks, but the implant usually needs a longer period to bond with the jawbone before the final replacement tooth is fitted. Bone grafting or other additional treatment can make the process longer.

Not everyone is immediately suitable for an implant. A dentist will usually assess the amount and condition of the jawbone, gum health, nearby teeth, bite, oral hygiene, smoking and relevant medical factors before deciding whether implant treatment is appropriate.

Mild to moderate soreness, swelling or bruising can occur after surgery and swelling is often greatest during the first couple of days. The gum commonly heals over the following one to two weeks, while the bone takes longer to heal around the implant. Some people may have a temporary tooth or other temporary replacement while the implant heals.

Dental implants can remain functional for many years, but they are not guaranteed to last forever. Their longevity can be affected by oral hygiene, gum health, smoking, bite forces, general health, regular dental care and the condition of the crown or other replacement tooth attached to the implant.

There is no single cost that applies to every patient. The total cost can depend on the number of teeth being replaced, the type of implant restoration, imaging and planning, the condition of the teeth and gums, whether bone grafting or other procedures are needed, and the materials used.

Depending on the situation, alternatives may include a dental bridge or a removable denture. A dentist can compare these options with you based on the missing tooth or teeth, nearby teeth, gum and bone condition, your preferences and the planned treatment.
